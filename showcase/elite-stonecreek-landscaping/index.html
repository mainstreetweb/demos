Our story

A family crew that stays
on your street for years

Stonecreek started in 2011 with one truck, a borrowed plate compactor and a single paver patio in the Boise North End. Fourteen seasons later we are still family-owned, still local, and still building the kind of yards our own neighbours point at.

We keep two design-build crews and one dedicated care crew. That means the person who draws your plan is on site the week it gets built, and the person who prunes your hydrangeas in year three already knows why they were planted there in the first place.

Everything we specify is chosen for the Treasure Valley — alkaline soil, hot dry Julys, hard freezes in January and a wind that comes down the foothills every spring. No catalogue plants that sulk after one winter.

Seasonal care

What your garden needs,
quarter by quarter

A Treasure Valley garden asks for different things in April than it does in October. This is the rhythm our care crew works to — and what we hand you in writing if you would rather do it yourself.

March – May

Wake the garden up before it wakes itself

Spring in Boise arrives fast and then freezes again. Everything we do in these weeks is about getting beds fed and irrigation running before the first genuinely hot day catches the garden unprepared.

  • Cut back & clean beds. Last year's perennial growth comes off, overwintering debris is removed, and the crowns get light.
  • Irrigation start-up. Charge the system, walk every zone, replace cracked heads and reset the controller for spring run times.
  • Fresh mulch, 2–3 inches. Holds moisture through July and keeps bindweed from getting a foothold.
  • Pre-emergent & slow-release feed. Applied to lawns and beds before soil temperatures climb.
  • Plant replacement. Anything that did not make it through winter gets replaced under warranty at this visit.

June – August

Hold the line through the hot months

July and August are where Treasure Valley gardens are won or lost. The work is less dramatic and much more about water discipline and steady deadheading.

  • Monthly irrigation audits. We check coverage and adjust run times as the heat builds instead of setting it once in May.
  • Deadheading & shearing. Keeps repeat-flowering perennials producing well into September.
  • Weed pressure control. Hand weeding in beds, spot treatment in gravel and joints.
  • Lawn height raised. Mowing higher shades the soil and cuts water use noticeably.
  • Pest and disease watch. Aphids, spider mite and fire blight get caught early on a scheduled visit.

September – November

The season that decides next spring

Fall is the most productive time in the garden calendar and the one homeowners most often skip. Roots keep growing for weeks after the top growth stops.

  • Core aeration & overseed. The single best thing you can do for a Boise lawn, done in the first half of September.
  • Best planting window of the year. Warm soil, cool air — new trees and shrubs establish far better than in spring.
  • Leaf & debris clean-up. Repeat visits rather than one late pass, so lawns are never smothered.
  • Irrigation blow-out. Compressed-air winterisation before the first hard freeze, with backflow drained.
  • Bulb planting. Tulips, alliums and daffodils go in for the following April.

December – February

Protect, prune and plan the next build

The garden is dormant but not idle. Winter is when structure shows, when pruning is safest, and when next season's projects get drawn.

  • Dormant structural pruning. Fruit trees and deciduous shrubs shaped while we can see the branch framework.
  • Anti-desiccant & wind protection. Burlap screening for young evergreens on exposed foothill lots.
  • Snow & ice care. Careful clearing away from beds, and no rock salt anywhere near planted areas.
  • Hardscape inspection. We look for joint sand loss, frost heave and drainage issues while they are easy to fix.
  • Design season. Winter is when we draw spring builds — the calendar for March fills by January.
